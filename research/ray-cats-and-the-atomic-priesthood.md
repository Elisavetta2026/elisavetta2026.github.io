If the last entry left you with the idea that language fails over long enough timescales, these two proposals are what you get when serious researchers take that failure seriously and keep going anyway.

## The ray cat solution

In 1984, semioticians Françoise Bastide and Paolo Fabbri proposed something that still gets treated as a punchline, despite being a genuinely rigorous piece of lateral thinking: breed cats whose fur changes color in the presence of radiation, then embed the association between "the cat changed color" and "danger, leave" into folklore, song, and myth — cultural transmission mechanisms that have demonstrably outlasted individual empires and languages.

The logic holds up better than the premise sounds. Domestic cats already live alongside humans across nearly every culture on Earth and have for thousands of years; the relationship itself is a durable transmission channel independent of any one civilization surviving. A folk tale — "when the cat's fur clouds over, you are standing somewhere the ancestors marked as forbidden" — doesn't need to be *believed literally* to do its job. It only needs to keep getting told, the way flood myths and plague warnings recur across unrelated cultures long after anyone remembers the specific event that started them.

Nobody bred the cats. The proposal was never meant as an engineering spec — it was a thought experiment about which channels of meaning actually persist, and it succeeded at that on its own terms, whether or not a single ray cat was ever born.

## The Atomic Priesthood

The second proposal came from Thomas Sebeok, the linguist tasked with formally assessing the nuclear semiotics problem for the Human Interference Task Force. Sebeok's conclusion was blunter than most: no physical marker, symbol, or inscription could be trusted to retain meaning for 10,000 years. What might survive instead was an institution — specifically, something modeled on the way religious orders have preserved ritual knowledge and taboo across millennia, often outliving the empires and languages around them.

His proposal, the "atomic priesthood," was a continuously self-perpetuating body — not necessarily religious in belief, but religious in *structure* — whose sole purpose was to maintain the knowledge of the waste sites and re-encode warnings for each new generation in whatever language and symbolism that generation could understand. Rituals, myths, and inherited taboo would carry the warning even after the original scientific language became meaningless, the same way religious prohibitions have carried behavioral rules (what not to eat, where not to go) long after most adherents could explain the rules' original justification.

It's an unsettling proposal, and Sebeok knew it — deliberately manufacturing a belief system to control future behavior raises obvious ethical questions about deception and inherited authority. But it's also the only proposal in this thread that treats "10,000 years" as a problem of *social continuity* rather than material durability, and that reframing is worth sitting with before moving to the next entry, which is about the way any warning — biological, institutional, or engraved in stone — can be undone by a single kind of person.
