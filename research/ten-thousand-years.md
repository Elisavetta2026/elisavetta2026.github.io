In 1981, the U.S. Department of Energy convened a panel to answer a question that sounds almost absurd once you say it out loud: how do you warn people, ten thousand years from now, to stay away from a hole in the ground?

The hole in question is the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, a deep geological repository for radioactive waste in New Mexico. Regulations required that any warning marker remain intelligible — able to deter, not just inform — for 10,000 years. To put that span in perspective: 10,000 years ago, no writing system existed anywhere on Earth. Agriculture was new. The most enduring human structures we can point to from that era are earthworks and cave paintings, and we still argue about what most of them meant to the people who made them.

## The problem isn't the material. It's the message.

Concrete degrades. Steel corrodes. Any physical marker eventually erodes, gets buried, or gets scavenged for its materials — a granite monolith is, to someone in a resource-scarce future, also just very good granite. So the panel's harder problem was never really engineering. It was semiotic: what sign, if any, retains a stable meaning across a span longer than every language, religion, and political system in recorded history has yet lasted?

Every language we have is younger than the warning needs to be. English is roughly 1,500 years old and already largely unreadable to itself at the far end of that range — a contemporary reader struggles with Old English. Cuneiform, one of the oldest writing systems we know of, is about 5,000 years old, and it took modern scholarship over a century to decode after it fell out of use. If the most durable writing systems in human history don't survive intact readability for even half of 10,000 years, text on a sign is not a communication strategy. It's a bet against everything we know about how meaning decays.

## What actually survives

The panel — and the researchers who picked up the problem afterward, including linguist Thomas Sebeok — started looking past language entirely, toward things that might carry meaning independent of any specific tongue: facial expressions of fear and disgust, the shape of thorns, the visceral wrongness of a landscape deliberately made ugly and lifeless. The idea wasn't to *explain* the danger. It was to make a place feel wrong to be in, on a level that doesn't require translation.

That reframing is the real subject of this thread. Once you stop treating the problem as "what sentence do we carve into the rock," and start treating it as "what feeling can we guarantee across ten millennia," you end up with much stranger, more interesting proposals — some of which never made it past the page, and one of which involves breeding a cat.
