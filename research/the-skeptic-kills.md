Every proposal in this thread — the ugly landscapes, the ray cats, the atomic priesthood — shares a quiet assumption: that a future reader, encountering the warning, will take it at face value. The uncomfortable insight that came out of the later nuclear semiotics discussions is that this assumption is the weakest part of the entire system, and it fails in a specific, predictable way.

## Forbidding a thing is an invitation

Human beings do not reliably treat "forbidden" as synonymous with "dangerous." Often it reads as the opposite — a signal that something valuable or significant is being hidden, gatekept, or hoarded. Tombs marked with curses got opened anyway, repeatedly, by people who assumed the curse was there to protect treasure, not warn about a real hazard. The more elaborate and forceful a warning is made, the more it can read, to a sufficiently doubtful visitor, as evidence that whoever built it had something worth finding.

This is the trap: an effective-looking deterrent and an effective-looking *lure* can be visually identical to someone outside the culture that built them. Monumental architecture, ominous language, imagery designed to provoke dread — these also happen to be exactly the features that mark a site as important in the archaeological and treasure-hunting imagination. You cannot reliably build "this place is worthless and dangerous, do not dig" without it resembling "this place was significant enough that someone went to enormous lengths to seal it."

## It only takes one

The other proposals in this thread rely on broad cultural transmission — a myth repeated widely enough, an institution maintained continuously enough, that the warning reaches nearly everyone. But a warning system doesn't fail when most people believe it. It fails the moment *one* determined skeptic decides the warning is superstition, priestly control, or a trick designed to keep them from what's underneath — and has the tools and motivation to test that theory directly.

This is sometimes summarized, a little bleakly, as "the skeptic kills the message": the message can be transmitted perfectly, believed by the overwhelming majority of the population, embedded in ritual and story exactly as the atomic priesthood proposal intended — and still fail, because containment depends on universal compliance, and belief systems have never once in recorded history achieved universal compliance. Every taboo has its violators. Every curse has its excavator.

None of this means the earlier proposals were wrong to attempt. It means the actual design target was never "guarantee no one ever investigates." Nothing can guarantee that, over 10,000 years, across every possible future culture. The real target — the one the panels eventually converged on — was closer to *slow down and mark, so that whoever does investigate does so with maximum information and minimum surprise.* That's a smaller, sadder goal than "keep everyone out forever." It's also the only one that survives contact with how people actually behave around forbidden things.
